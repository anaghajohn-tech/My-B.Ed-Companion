Cognitive Psychology and Piaget

Part A in all 3 papers.

Cognitive psychology: meaning and importance 2+4

The study of mental processes: perception, attention, memory, thinking, problem solving and language.

Importance

  • Explains how learners take in, store and use information
  • Guides lesson design, such as chunking and revision for memory
  • Develops thinking and problem-solving skills
  • Helps teachers understand individual differences in learning
  • Supports teaching of study skills and metacognition
Piaget's stages of cognitive development 6

Key ideas

Schema (mental structure), assimilation (fit new info into a schema), accommodation (change the schema), equilibration (balance).

Four stages

  • Sensorimotor (0–2 yrs): learns through senses and actions; object permanence develops
  • Preoperational (2–7): symbols and language; egocentric; cannot yet conserve
  • Concrete operational (7–11): logical thinking about concrete things; conservation, classification, reversibility
  • Formal operational (11+): abstract and hypothetical thinking; scientific reasoning

Classroom implications

  • Use hands-on material for younger learners
  • Match tasks to the learner's stage; give problems that stretch it

Vygotsky's Socio-cultural Theory

Part A / Part B in all 3 papers.

Theory and ZPD 5

Learning is first social, then individual. Knowledge is built through interaction with others and through cultural tools, mainly language.

  • ZPD (Zone of Proximal Development): the gap between what a child can do alone and what they can do with help
  • MKO (More Knowledgeable Other): teacher, parent or peer who guides
  • Scaffolding: temporary support, removed as the learner gains skill
  • Private speech turns into inner thought
Relevance to teaching and learning 5
  • Teacher acts as guide, not just a giver of information
  • Group work, peer tutoring and reciprocal teaching
  • Scaffolding, hints and questioning within the ZPD
  • Dialogue and discussion in class
  • Use of the learner's culture and language
Socio-cultural conditions in learning 5
  • Culture, language, family, community and peers shape what and how a child learns
  • Social tools (language, symbols, technology) carry knowledge
  • Learners from different backgrounds need examples from their own world
  • Teachers should use local culture, home language and group work

Constructivism

Appears in all 3 papers, several ways.

What is constructivism? Learning as construction of knowledge 2 / 5

Learners are active. They build their own understanding by linking new experiences with prior knowledge (schemas), rather than receiving ready-made facts.

  • Prior knowledge is the starting point
  • Learning happens by doing, exploring and reflecting
  • Social interaction helps shape meaning
  • Understanding is personal and can differ between learners
Role of teacher in a constructivist classroom 4–5
  • Facilitator and guide, not only a lecturer
  • Creates real-life problems and rich tasks
  • Draws out prior knowledge; asks open questions
  • Encourages discussion, group work and reflection
  • Provides resources and scaffolding
  • Assesses the process, not only the answer
Characteristics of a constructivist classroom 3–4
  • Learner-centred and activity-based
  • Collaborative learning
  • Real-world, problem-based tasks
  • Multiple viewpoints are welcomed
  • Flexible curriculum that follows learner interests
  • Assessment through projects and portfolios
How do you motivate learners in the construction of knowledge? 5
  • Link topics to learners' lives and interests
  • Use curiosity-raising questions and puzzles
  • Give choice and challenging but achievable tasks
  • Collaborative projects, praise and constructive feedback
  • Let learners see their own progress
Characteristics of a constructivist teacher; implications for curriculum and pedagogy 4–5

Teacher

  • Facilitator, guide and co-learner
  • Respects learners' ideas and prior knowledge
  • Asks open questions; encourages inquiry and reflection
  • Flexible, patient, uses real-life tasks

Implications

  • Curriculum: theme-based, linked to life, flexible, with less rote content
  • Pedagogy: activity, group work, projects, problem solving, discussion
  • Assessment: portfolios, projects and observation

Discovery Learning and Bruner

Appears in all 3 papers.

Discovery learning and its learning cycle 1+4 / 2+4

Learners find out concepts and rules themselves through exploration and problem solving. The teacher guides rather than tells (Jerome Bruner).

Stages of the learning cycle

  • Engage / stimulation: a puzzling problem or question
  • Explore: collect data, try things out, observe
  • Explain / concept formation: the learner states the pattern; the teacher names the concept
  • Elaborate / application: use the concept in new situations
  • Evaluate: check understanding and give feedback
Some books give a 3-stage version: exploration, concept introduction, concept application. Use whichever your class notes follow.
Bruner's principles for assessing quality of learning 4–5
  • Structure: does the learner grasp the key ideas, not just facts?
  • Transfer: can they apply learning to new problems?
  • Discovery and problem solving: can they reason it out themselves?
  • Readiness and motivation: is the task at the right level and interesting?
  • Sequence (spiral curriculum): do ideas build on earlier ones?
  • Feedback: is information about performance given at the right time?

Learning Styles

Part A in all 3 papers.

Meaning and types 2+2 / 2+4

A learning style is a learner's preferred way of taking in and processing information.

Types (VARK)

  • Visual: learns from charts, diagrams, colours. Example: mind maps.
  • Auditory: learns by listening and discussing. Example: lectures, group talk.
  • Read/write: learns from text and notes. Example: note-making, lists.
  • Kinesthetic: learns by doing. Example: experiments, models, role play.

Kolb's styles: diverging, assimilating, converging, accommodating.

Factors affecting learning style 4–6
  • Heredity and age; brain dominance
  • Personality and temperament
  • Culture and family background
  • Prior experience and education
  • Teaching methods used
  • Environment: light, noise, seating
  • Motivation and interests
Importance in the teaching-learning process 2
  • Teachers can match methods to learners
  • Improves understanding, motivation and results
  • Helps learners study in their own best way

Teaching Styles

Part B in all 3 papers.

Meaning and factors 2+3

A teaching style is the teacher's typical way of teaching and managing a class: how they present, interact and assess. Examples: authoritarian, democratic, laissez-faire; or Grasha's expert, formal authority, personal model, facilitator, delegator.

Factors affecting it

  • Teacher's personality, beliefs and training
  • Subject and content
  • Age and level of learners
  • Class size and available resources
  • School culture, curriculum and exams
  • Teacher's experience
Types of teaching style; effect on learning; link with learning style 4–5

Types

  • Authoritarian: teacher controls; learners follow
  • Democratic: shared decisions, discussion
  • Laissez-faire: learners largely free
  • Grasha: expert, formal authority, personal model, facilitator, delegator

Effect on learning

  • Shapes motivation, participation, anxiety and results

Link with learning style

Learning goes best when teaching style matches, or is varied to suit, the learners' styles.

Effective Teaching and Principles of Teaching

Appears in all 3 papers.

Effective teaching and its components 2+3 / 2+2

Teaching that helps learners achieve the planned learning outcomes with understanding and interest.

Components

  • Clear objectives and planning
  • Subject knowledge
  • Suitable methods and teaching aids
  • Clear communication and questioning
  • Motivation and learner engagement
  • Classroom management and a positive climate
  • Assessment and feedback
Parameters of effective teaching 6
  • Clarity of objectives and lesson structure
  • Mastery of content
  • Learner participation
  • Use of examples, TLM and ICT
  • Quality of questioning and feedback
  • Attention to individual differences
  • Learning outcomes and reflection
Principles of teaching 5
  • Known to unknown; simple to complex; concrete to abstract; whole to part
  • Learning by doing (activity)
  • Motivation and interest
  • Individual differences
  • Revision and reinforcement
  • Democratic, flexible approach
Identification of teaching skills 4–5
  • Observe lessons (peer or supervisor) with a checklist
  • Microteaching and feedback
  • Skills: introducing the lesson, explaining, questioning, using the board and aids, reinforcement, stimulus variation, closure
  • Analyse recordings; self-reflection

Hemispheres of the Brain

Part B in all 3 papers.

Thinking activities of right and left hemispheres 5

Left hemisphere

  • Logic, analysis, step-by-step thinking
  • Language, reading, writing, maths
  • Facts, detail and sequence

Right hemisphere

  • Imagination, creativity, intuition
  • Art, music, spatial and visual thinking
  • Big picture, emotions, recognising faces

The two sides are linked by the corpus callosum and work together.

Implication

Use both: logical tasks and creative, visual activities.

Dyslexia and Dyscalculia

Dyslexia in Part A; dyscalculia in Part B, all 3 papers.

Dyslexia: meaning, characteristics, remedies 2+5+3

A specific learning disability in reading: difficulty decoding words, spelling and reading fluently, despite normal intelligence.

Characteristics

  • Confuses similar letters (b/d, p/q)
  • Slow, hesitant reading; skips words
  • Poor spelling; trouble with sounds and rhymes
  • Difficulty copying, sequencing and handwriting
  • Avoids reading aloud; low confidence

Remedial measures

  • Multisensory teaching: see, hear, trace, say
  • Phonics practice, individual attention
  • Extra time, audio books, large print
  • Early identification, remedial teacher, counselling
  • Parent involvement; praise for effort
Dyscalculia: meaning and characteristics 2+3

A specific learning disability in mathematics: difficulty understanding numbers and arithmetic.

  • Weak number sense; trouble counting and comparing
  • Mixes up signs (+, −, ×) and place value
  • Cannot recall maths facts, tables
  • Difficulty with time, money, direction and word problems
  • Maths anxiety

Help

Concrete objects, visual aids, small steps and real-life examples.

Dysgraphia: meaning, characteristics, remedies 2+3+3

A learning disability in writing: difficulty forming letters, spacing and putting thoughts into writing.

Characteristics

  • Illegible, uneven handwriting; slow writing
  • Poor spacing and spelling; mixes cases
  • Tires quickly; avoids writing

Remedies

  • Tracing and letter-formation practice
  • Pencil grips, lined paper, extra time
  • Typing or oral answers where allowed
  • Fine-motor exercises; encouragement

Creativity

Part B in all 3 papers.

Creative children, characteristics, fostering 1+4 / 2+3

Creativity: the ability to produce ideas that are original and useful. Torrance's parts: fluency, flexibility, originality, elaboration.

Characteristics of creative children

  • Curious; ask many questions
  • Imaginative and original
  • Flexible, open-minded
  • Independent, willing to take risks
  • Good sense of humour

Fostering creativity

  • Open-ended questions and brainstorming
  • Art, music, drama, projects
  • Freedom to try; no ridicule of ideas
  • Problem-solving tasks and puzzles
  • Appreciate and display original work
Educational programmes for creative children 4
  • Open-ended, project-based tasks and brainstorming
  • Clubs: art, drama, science, writing
  • Exhibitions, competitions, fairs
  • Flexible timetable and enrichment activities
  • Guidance and mentoring

Emotional Development

Part B in all 3 papers.

Factors influencing emotional development 5
  • Heredity, health and maturation
  • Family: parenting style and home atmosphere
  • School and teachers; peers
  • Culture, media and society
  • Experiences of success and failure
  • Personality
Why teachers need to know it; effect on learning 5
  • Adolescence brings mood swings and stress; teachers can respond with understanding
  • Positive emotions improve attention, memory and motivation; anxiety and anger block learning
  • Helps build a safe, friendly classroom
  • Allows early support and counselling
  • Helps manage behaviour and relationships
Meaning and process of emotional development 2+4

The growth of the ability to feel, understand, express and control emotions, from simple reactions in infancy to mature control in adulthood.

  • Infancy: basic emotions (joy, fear, anger)
  • Childhood: learns to name and regulate feelings
  • Adolescence: intense, changing emotions; identity concerns
  • Shaped by maturation and learning through family, peers and school

Gifted Children

Appeared in 2023 and 2025.

Meaning, characteristics, programmes 2+3

A gifted child shows very high ability (often IQ 130 and above) in one or more areas.

Characteristics

  • Learns quickly; excellent memory
  • Wide vocabulary, curiosity, strong reasoning
  • Long attention for interesting tasks
  • Creative; may get bored in regular class

Educational programmes

  • Acceleration (skip grades)
  • Enrichment: projects, extra challenges
  • Ability grouping and special schools (e.g. Navodaya)
  • Mentoring, Olympiads and counselling

Motivation, Learning In/Out of School, Instruction Strategies

From the teacher's important-questions list.

Learners' motivation and how to motivate students 5

Motivation is the inner drive that starts, directs and sustains learning. Intrinsic (interest, curiosity) and extrinsic (rewards, marks).

  • Set clear, achievable goals
  • Link lessons to learners' lives
  • Use variety: activities, aids, games
  • Give praise, timely feedback and recognition
  • Encourage cooperation and healthy competition
  • Create a safe, friendly climate
Learning in and outside school 5
  • In school: formal, planned, syllabus-based, teacher-led, assessed, gives certificates
  • Outside school: informal, through family, peers, community, media, play and work; flexible and lifelong
  • Both are needed: connect classroom content to real-life experience
Classroom instruction strategies 5
  • Lecture with questioning; demonstration
  • Discussion and cooperative learning
  • Inquiry, discovery and problem solving
  • Project and activity-based learning
  • Concept mapping; use of ICT
  • Differentiated instruction and scaffolding

Educationally Backward Children

Appeared in 2022 and 2025.

Meaning, causes, remedial measures 2+4+4

Children whose achievement is clearly below what is expected for their age and class.

Causes

  • Low intelligence or learning disability
  • Poor health and nutrition
  • Poverty, uneducated parents, poor home environment
  • Poor teaching, large classes, irregular attendance
  • Emotional problems; lack of motivation

Remedial measures

  • Diagnose the weak areas
  • Remedial teaching in small groups
  • Individual attention and suitable TLM
  • Motivation, praise and counselling
  • Health check-ups; involve parents
Characteristics and educational programmes 4+4

Characteristics

  • Low marks in most subjects; weak basics
  • Slow learner, low attention, poor memory
  • Irregular attendance, low self-confidence
  • Dislike of school, behaviour problems

Programmes

  • Remedial classes and bridge courses
  • Peer tutoring, activity-based learning
  • Slow-paced, individualised teaching
  • Counselling and parent meetings
