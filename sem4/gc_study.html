Guidance and Counselling

Every topic asked in the 5 papers (2018 – 2024), explained point by point. Points marked ★ come from the official March 2018 scheme of valuation (N. Lokanath, University College of Education, Bangalore University).

Guidance

★ marks points taken from the official 2018 scheme of valuation.

Meaning and nature of guidance 2+4

Guidance is the help given to a person to understand himself and his surroundings so that he can make wise choices, solve problems and adjust well. Think of a guide on a mountain trail: the guide does not carry you up; he shows the paths and you decide and walk.

Nature

  • Continuous process: not a one-time act; it goes on through school years and life.
  • For everyone: not only for problem cases; every student needs it at some time.
  • Help to self-help: the aim is to make the person able to guide himself.
  • Preventive as well as corrective: it tries to stop problems before they grow.
  • Based on individual differences: each student is helped according to ability and need.
  • Democratic and co-operative: the student, teachers, parents and counsellor work together; nothing is forced.
  • Comprehensive: covers educational, vocational and personal areas.
  • Scientific and professional: uses tests, records and trained personnel.
Need and importance of guidance in schools 4 / 2+3
  • Individual differences: students differ in ability, interest and pace; guidance helps each to be taught suitably.
  • Choice of courses and subjects: after class X students must choose streams; wrong choice causes failure and drop-out.
  • Vocational choice: the world of work is wide and changing; students need information about careers.
  • Adolescent problems: body and emotional changes, peer pressure, stress and identity worries.
  • Wastage and stagnation: guidance reduces failure and school leaving.
  • Changing society: joint families breaking, urban life, media; children get less personal advice at home.
  • Mental health and discipline: early help reduces tension, indiscipline and delinquency.
  • Use of leisure and talents: helps develop hobbies and special abilities.

Benefits to students and teachers

  • Students: know themselves, choose wisely, adjust better, study better, face exams with less stress
  • Teachers: understand pupils better, handle discipline problems, teach with more insight
  • School: better results and a healthy climate; Parents: are guided about their child
Principles of guidance ★ 5

Principles are the rules that keep guidance sound.

  • Respect for individual differences: treat each person as unique.
  • Universal: meant for all, not only problem students.
  • Individualisation: help is fitted to the person.
  • Anticipatory: looks ahead and prevents problems.
  • Life-long process: continues from childhood to old age.
  • Code of ethics: follow confidentiality and respect.
  • Co-operation, not compulsion: the student accepts help willingly.
  • Self-development: aims at the ability to guide oneself.
  • Planning for the future: prepares the person for later life.
  • Specialised job: needs trained workers.
  • Inter-related and flexible: linked with all school activities and open to change.
  • Based on a true concept of the individual: treats the person as a whole.
Principles of organising guidance services in schools 5
  • Clear objectives based on pupil needs
  • Trained and committed personnel
  • Co-operation of head, teachers, parents and community
  • Adequate time, rooms, tools and records
  • Service for all students; flexible and continuous
  • Confidentiality of information
  • Regular evaluation and follow-up
Educational, vocational and personal guidance ★ 2+8 / 2+3
  • Educational guidance: helps students choose courses, schools and subjects; gives information on institutions and scholarships; helps adjustment to the curriculum and study methods; helps with problems of learning and further schooling; assists educational planning.
  • Vocational guidance: helps students acquire knowledge of occupations; discover their skills; gives information about different jobs; gives chances to try out courses; helps vocational planning and adjustment to the working situation.
  • Personal guidance: helps with emotional problems, health, relationships, values and social adjustment; builds self-confidence.

Importance of educational guidance in secondary schools: students face a turning point at class X, so correct subject choice, good study habits and reduced failure matter greatly.

Guidance vs counselling ★ 4 / 2+3

Guidance is the wider help; counselling is a close, personal part of it.

  • Scope: guidance is broad and covers education, career and personal life; counselling is narrower and deals with a specific personal problem.
  • Who leads: in guidance the guide often directs and informs; in counselling the control moves toward the client, who makes the decisions.
  • Numbers: guidance can be given to groups; counselling is usually one-to-one.
  • Nature: guidance is preventive and informative; counselling is deeper, emotional and remedial.
  • Relationship: counselling depends on a trusting personal relationship; guidance may be impersonal (a talk, a leaflet).
  • Person giving help: teacher or guide for guidance; trained counsellor for counselling.
Role of guidance in national development ★ 4
  • Economic development: right persons in right jobs
  • Stops loss caused by mismatch between education and vacancies
  • Develops social values and a well-adjusted, co-operative society
  • Reduces health problems and unemployment
  • Helps raise literacy
  • Builds dignity of labour and constructive living
  • Links education, employment and the economic progress of the country

Counselling and Counselling Skills

Counselling is in every paper.

Meaning of counselling ★ and its objectives 2+3

Counselling is a face-to-face helping relationship in which a trained counsellor helps a client understand himself and solve his own problem.

  • Ruth Strong: counselling is a face-to-face relationship in which growth takes place in the counsellor as well as the counselee.
  • Myres: counselling is a relationship between two individuals in which one gives a certain kind of assistance to another.

Objectives

  • Self-understanding and acceptance
  • Help in making decisions and solving problems
  • Adjustment to school, home and society
  • Change of harmful behaviour
  • Mental health and personal growth
  • Developing the client's potential
Directive vs non-directive counselling ★ 2+4 / 3 / 4

Directive (counsellor-centred): the counsellor takes the major responsibility for solving the problem; he collects facts, finds the cause and advises. Non-directive (client-centred, permissive): the client plays the main role; the counsellor listens, accepts and helps the client find his own answers (Carl Rogers).

  • Who leads: directive: counsellor; non-directive: client.
  • Focus: directive: the problem; non-directive: the person.
  • Counsellor role: directive: expert who advises; non-directive: warm listener who reflects.
  • Client role: directive: follows advice; non-directive: explores and decides.
  • Method: directive: analysis, diagnosis, advice; non-directive: free talk, emotional release, self-insight.
  • Best for: directive: informational, educational or decision problems; non-directive: emotional and personal problems.
Steps of directive counselling ★ 5

Think of a doctor: gather facts, find the illness, predict, treat, follow up.

  • 1. Analysis: collect information about the client from all sources (records, tests, interview, parents).
  • 2. Synthesis: organise and summarise the information to show strengths and weaknesses.
  • 3. Diagnosis: find the causes of the problem.
  • 4. Prognosis: predict how the problem may develop and what solutions are likely.
  • 5. Counselling: take the steps that help the person adjust: advice, information, plan.
  • 6. Follow-up: check progress and the effectiveness of the counselling; help again if needed.
Steps of non-directive counselling ★ 5
  • Conversation and gathering of information with the counselee
  • Probing into the situation of the counselee
  • Emotional release through frank expression of feelings
  • Discussion in which suggestions are given only indirectly
  • Project formulation with free discussion of the plan
  • Project implementation and evaluation
Counselling skills: rapport building ★ and others 5

Skills are what the counsellor does to make the helping effective.

  • Rapport building: creating a relationship of trust and empathy; a meaningful connection with the client. Tools: empathy, active listening and the use of open-ended and closed-ended questions. Open-ended questions ("How did you feel?") encourage the client to talk and give examples. Closed-ended questions ("Did you go to school?") get specific facts and narrow the topic. Rapport is the most powerful tool for building a trusting relationship.
  • Active listening: giving full attention, nodding, noticing feelings and not interrupting.
  • Empathy: understanding the client's feelings from his point of view, like walking in his shoes.
  • Questioning: using open and closed questions wisely.
  • Reflecting and paraphrasing: repeating in your own words what the client said so he feels heard.
  • Probing and clarifying: asking gently for more detail.
  • Silence and encouragement: giving time to think; small prompts such as "I see".
  • Summarising and referral: closing with key points; sending serious cases to a specialist.
Qualities of a counsellor ★ and professional ethics 4 / 5

Qualities

  • Magnetic, pleasant personality and good personal adjustment
  • Good interpersonal relationships, communication and listening skills
  • Professional competence and dedication; leadership
  • Empathy, acceptance, genuineness and positive feeling for the client
  • Broad-minded, wide interests, sense of humour, a sound philosophy of life

Code of ethics

  • Confidentiality: client information is kept private (except where there is danger to life).
  • Respect and non-discrimination: treat every client with dignity.
  • Competence: work within one's training; refer when needed.
  • Informed consent: explain the process and obtain agreement.
  • No exploitation: no misuse of trust or dual relationships.
  • Honest records and continuous learning.
Student problems needing counselling ★; problems of schools without counselling 2+4+4

Student problems: adjustment problems, transition from school or college, alcohol and other drugs, anger management, anxiety and stress, mood changes and depression, eating concerns, low self-esteem, body image, grief and loss, relationship conflicts, gender and sexual concerns, spirituality, suicidal feelings.

Problems of schools without counselling: students' emotional problems go unnoticed; poor results and drop-out; discipline and behaviour problems grow; wrong subject and career choices; teachers overburdened; weak school-home contact.

Solutions (any two problems): personal counselling, family counselling, medical help when a psychiatric or neurological problem is diagnosed, behavioural contracts (the student takes responsibility for conduct), house rules, relaxation techniques.

Guidance Services and Their Organisation

Components, purposes and types asked in almost every paper.

Purposes of organising guidance and counselling services ★ 5
  • Help students with normal developmental problems and through temporary crises
  • Spot signs of disturbed behaviour early and refer serious cases to specialists
  • Improve communication between school and home
  • Give needed information and help in making wise choices
  • Improve self-understanding; make students self-sufficient and independent
  • Help students adjust to change and a new environment
  • Make effective use of potentials and talents; promote personal and professional development
  • Help adjustment in school, home and society; help delinquents; appraise pupils' abilities
Guidance services in schools ★ 5

Each service is one way the school helps.

  • Orientation service: introduces new students to the school, rules and facilities.
  • Student information service: collects data about each pupil and gives information on courses and careers.
  • Educational, vocational and personal guidance services.
  • Placement service: helps students get suitable jobs or courses.
  • Referral service: sends special cases to doctors, psychologists and agencies.
  • Remedial service: teaching for those weak in subjects.
  • Health and social services.
  • Follow-up service: checks how students have done after help or after leaving school.
  • Research and evaluation service: studies the results and improves the programme.
Types and organisation of services: centralised, decentralised ★ 5 / 2½+2½
  • Centralised: the entire responsibility rests on trained guidance personnel. Merit: expert help. Limit: costly; fewer staff reach all pupils.
  • Decentralised: the responsibility rests on the teachers. Merit: every teacher knows his pupils. Limit: teachers may lack training and time.
  • Combination of both: teachers and experts work together. This is the best and the most commonly followed type.

Organisational set-up

  • Head of the institution: overall leader
  • Guidance committee: plans and reviews
  • Counsellor / career master: trains and co-ordinates
  • Class teachers: first-level help and records
  • Doctor, parents and community persons: support
Basic components of guidance and counselling services ★ 5

The scheme lists five components:

  • Organisational set-up: the plan of who does what: committee, counsellor, teachers.
  • Counselling centre: a room or block where students meet the counsellor in privacy.
  • Counselling committee: head, counsellor, teachers and parent members who plan and review.
  • Tools for counselling services: tests, cumulative records, questionnaires, interviews, observation, case study.
  • Requirements: trained staff, space, funds, time, records and a confidential atmosphere.
Counselling centre: meaning and how to equip ★ 3+2 / 1+4

A centre that offers service to the individual who has a problem and needs professional help; it gives specialised help, personality development training and exceptional guidance.

  • A quiet, private and comfortable room (therapy room)
  • Well-trained counsellors, consultants and psychometrists; links with psychotherapists
  • Psychological tests, a psychology lab, record files and career information books
  • Furniture and aids for individual and group work
  • Therapy block and a schedule that students can reach easily
Career resource centre: significance and organisation ★ 5

Significance

  • Helps students picture and plan their future and understand career-related issues
  • Trained staff help with career concerns and work with employers
  • Keeps up with career trends and helps students find what they want to do
  • A good place to meet other students who share the same worries

How to organise

  • Decide the venue
  • Collect career resources: books, articles, e-resources
  • Plan programmes: workshops, career talks, exhibitions
  • Build a network with other organisations
  • Offer one-to-one interaction
  • Set up a web presence

Tests, Records and Techniques

Used by the counsellor to know the student.

Standardised and non-standardised tests: role ★ 5+5

Standardised tests have fixed content, instructions and scoring, with norms from large groups (reliable and valid). Non-standardised (teacher-made) tests are made by the teacher for a class.

Role

  • Find achievement and attainment after teaching
  • Find difficulties and weaknesses in learning and suggest solutions
  • Discover potentials and fulfil needs; help in all-round development
  • Find causes of low achievement
  • Develop study habits; mould behaviour
  • Support continuous evaluation and improve scholastic achievement

Aptitude and achievement tests: aptitude shows the ability to learn in future, achievement shows what has been learnt. Together they help the counsellor advise on courses and careers.

Projective techniques ★ 1+4 / 5+2+3

The person is shown vague material (inkblots, pictures, incomplete sentences) and gives meaning to it; in doing so he projects his own feelings, attitudes and self-concept. It is like seeing shapes in clouds: what you see tells about you.

  • Examples: Rorschach inkblot test, Thematic Apperception Test, sentence completion, word association, drawings.
  • Advantages: uncovers feelings, beliefs and motivation that clients find hard to put in words; reduces anxiety by giving the situation some structure; shows the private world and inner view of the person; "breaks the ice"; gives a view of overall functioning.
  • Disadvantages: scoring is subjective; needs trained persons; time-consuming; reliability is lower; results can be misread.
Diagnostic test ★ 2+3

A test given to find the strengths and weaknesses of a learner and the reason for the weakness. Like a doctor's check-up before treatment.

Advantages

  • Shows current knowledge, skills and capabilities
  • Identifies strengths, weaknesses and errors
  • Spots students at risk (for example in reading)
  • Helps plan better lessons and remedial teaching
  • Helps the counsellor find the cause of low achievement
Cumulative record and autobiography 3+4+3

A cumulative record is a continuing record of a pupil's development over the school years: personal and family data, health, marks, test scores, co-curricular work, interests, behaviour and teachers' remarks.

Helps guidance because

  • It gives a full picture of the student in one place
  • Counsellor sees growth and sudden changes over time
  • Helps in choosing courses, scholarships and careers
  • Useful in parent-teacher meetings and referral
  • Gives continuity when teachers change

Autobiography: the student writes about his own life, interests, problems and hopes. Advantages: free self-expression, shows attitudes and hidden problems, cheap and easy, builds rapport and gives material for counselling.

Interview and questionnaire 5+5 / 5
  • Interview: a purposeful face-to-face conversation. Advantages: personal contact, flexible, gives depth, builds rapport, behaviour can be observed. Limits: time-consuming, interviewer bias.
  • Questionnaire: a set of written questions answered by the person. Advantages: many students at once, saves time and cost, honest answers when anonymous. Limits: no chance to clarify; some do not return it.
Observation, case study, sociometry ★ 3+2 / 2+3 / 4
  • Observation (uses): natural and more scientific; collects data about behaviour; needs no special tool; useful for children; helps diagnosis and analysis of problems; improves communication.
  • Observation (demerits): time-consuming; needs trained observers; shows only outer behaviour; subjective interpretation; observer bias; cannot always be natural; two persons cannot be observed at once.
  • Case study: a detailed study of one person to understand a problem: collect data, find causes, plan help, follow up. Advantages: depth, shows causes, suits special problems.
  • Sociometry: shows who is liked or rejected in a group (sociogram). Uses: find isolates and leaders, plan seating and groups, improve social adjustment.

Group Guidance and Career Programmes

Career talk and class talk in every paper.

Individual guidance vs group guidance ★; measures 2½+2½ / 2+4+4

Individual guidance is given to one person about his own particular problems, circumstances or plans. Group guidance is a service given to a small or large group to solve their common problems.

  • Number: one student / many students.
  • Problems: personal and private / common to all.
  • Time and cost: more time per student / saves time and effort.
  • Privacy: high / lower.
  • Method: interview, counselling, observation / talks, discussion, role play, orientation.

Measures while giving guidance

  • Build rapport and a warm environment
  • Educate the student about his strengths and weaknesses
  • Assist in the selection of courses, subjects and career
  • Give motivation; help improve attitude and behaviour
  • Focus on both individual and common problems; keep observation
  • Help develop good habits
Career talk and class talk ★ 1+4 / 5 / 2+3

Career talk: a talk by a professional or counsellor about a career. Class talk: a talk to the whole class on common school matters.

  • Career talk benefits: saves counsellor time; all students see the world of work early; helps choose a future profession; large numbers can be guided; links school to the community; students meet experts; makes parents guidance-minded; gives realistic information.
  • Class talk topics: effective study methods, role of co-curricular activities, importance of homework, time management, preparing for examinations; builds confidence, reduces monotony and focuses attention on common problems.
  • Difference: career talk is about occupations and is given by outside experts; class talk is about school life and study matters and is usually given by the teacher or counsellor.

How to organise a talk

  • Decide the aim and target class
  • Choose and invite a suitable speaker; brief him
  • Fix date, time and place; inform students
  • Conduct the session; give time for questions
  • Take feedback and follow up
Career exhibition ★ 2+3 / 1+4

A career exhibition (job or career fair) is an event where employers, colleges and professionals meet students and give information about careers.

Advantages to students

  • Chance to ask questions and talk about their skills
  • Information about qualifications and special training
  • Knowledge about income, benefits, promotion and working conditions
  • Ways of finding a job and sources of further information
  • Making useful contacts (networking)

How to organise

  • Form a committee and decide objectives
  • Fix date and venue
  • Invite employers, colleges and training institutes; allot stalls
  • Publicise to students and parents
  • Programme: stalls, talks, demonstrations, counselling desk
  • Collect feedback and follow up
Group counselling in secondary schools 5

A small group (6-10 students) with a common concern meets a trained counsellor for several sessions.

  • Identify students with similar concerns (exam anxiety, peer pressure, study skills)
  • Plan the sessions and set ground rules, including confidentiality
  • The counsellor facilitates discussion, role play and sharing
  • Students support each other and learn from others' experience
  • Close with a summary, feedback and follow-up; refer individuals who need private help

Adolescent Problems

Aggression and addiction asked almost every year.

Behavioural problems of adolescents ★ 2
  • Addiction: dependence on tobacco, alcohol or drugs.
  • Aggression: anger, fighting, bullying, defiance.
  • Anxiety: constant worry, exam fear, panic.
  • Truancy: staying away from school without permission.
  • ADHD: inattention, restlessness and impulsive behaviour.
Aggressive behaviour: causes and measures ★ 2+4+4

Aggression is behaviour that harms or threatens others in words or action.

Causes

  • Traumatic events: death or illness of a loved one, harassment, ongoing fights and discord in the family
  • Abuse: physical or sexual abuse; harsh or shaming words
  • Psychiatric disorders: bipolar disorder, depression, schizophrenia, post-traumatic stress
  • Medical conditions: brain damage, epilepsy, mental retardation, Tourette syndrome
  • ADHD, addiction, peer pressure and low self-esteem

Measures

  • Personal counselling
  • Family counselling
  • Medication when a psychiatric or neurological problem is diagnosed
  • Behavioural contracts: make them take responsibility for their behaviour
  • House rules; relaxation techniques
Addiction: causes and counselling measures ★ 4 / 2+3

Causes

  • Brain: weak functioning of the frontal lobe; need for immediate gratification
  • Weakness, impulsive nature, thrill seeking, curiosity, early exposure
  • Home: bad home environment; lack of parental care or over-pampering; no chance to talk to parents
  • Peer and social influence; environment and culture
  • Genetics; stress or traumatic experiences; lack of social support

Measures while counselling

  • Build trust; listen without judging
  • Find and work on the cause (stress, peers, family)
  • Help the student see harm and set own goals (motivational talk)
  • Involve parents; refer to a de-addiction centre or doctor when needed
  • Teach life skills: refusal, coping with stress
  • Encourage sports, hobbies and healthy friends; follow-up
ADHD: meaning and measures 1+4

Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder is a condition in which a child shows long-lasting inattention, over-activity and impulsiveness that disturb learning.

Measures

  • Medical check-up; treatment only under a doctor
  • Counselling and behaviour therapy for the child; guidance for parents
  • Clear and simple instructions; short tasks with breaks
  • Seat near the teacher, away from distractions
  • Daily routine, praise and rewards for good behaviour; avoid punishment
  • Physical activity and enough sleep; teacher-parent co-operation
