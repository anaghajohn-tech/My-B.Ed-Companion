Evaluation, CCE and Examinations

Evaluation and CCE appear in every paper.

Assessment, measurement and evaluation; their difference 5

Assessment is collecting information about how well a student is learning (tests, observation, projects). Measurement gives that information a number (a student scored 60 out of 100). Evaluation judges the value of it against objectives ("this is good; the student needs help in fractions"). Think of a thermometer: it measures 39°; the doctor evaluates that it is a fever.

  • Nature: measurement is quantitative (numbers); evaluation is quantitative and qualitative.
  • Scope: measurement is narrow (one trait, one test); evaluation is wide (whole development of the child).
  • Purpose: measurement only tells how much; evaluation tells how good and what to do next.
  • Objectives: measurement can be done without objectives; evaluation is based on objectives.
  • Decision: measurement gives data; evaluation makes judgments and decisions.
  • Relation: evaluation = measurement + value judgment.
Meaning, purpose and importance of evaluation 2+4+4

Evaluation is the continuous, systematic process of finding out how far the educational objectives are being achieved and what the value of the programme is.

  • Improves learning: students know what they have learnt and what is weak.
  • Improves teaching: the teacher learns if the method worked and changes it.
  • Diagnosis and remedy: finds learning difficulties early.
  • Motivation: results and feedback encourage effort.
  • Guidance: helps in choosing courses and careers.
  • Selection and promotion: gives a fair basis for grades and certificates.
  • Curriculum improvement: shows which content or objective needs revision.
  • Accountability: tells parents and society how the school is doing.
Types of evaluation; formative vs summative 4 / 2½+2½ / 5
  • Placement evaluation: before teaching, to know the starting level.
  • Formative evaluation: during teaching, to give feedback and improve learning. Examples: class tests, questioning, assignments. It is for learning.
  • Diagnostic evaluation: finds the reasons for repeated difficulty.
  • Summative evaluation: at the end of a unit, term or year, to grade and certify. Examples: annual examination. It is of learning.

Formative vs summative

  • Time: during the course / at the end.
  • Purpose: improve learning / judge achievement and give grades.
  • Frequency: frequent and short / occasional and long.
  • Weightage: low stakes / high stakes.
  • Feedback: immediate / late.
Scholastic and co-scholastic assessment 2½+2½
  • Scholastic: assessment of academic subjects: tests, assignments, projects, practicals. Purpose: to know subject knowledge and skills.
  • Co-scholastic: assessment of the other areas of growth: life skills, attitudes and values, sports, art and music, health and hygiene, leadership, discipline. Purpose: to develop the whole child, not only marks; usually graded on a scale and by observation.
Principles and process of evaluation 5+5 / 10

Principles

  • Based on objectives: what is evaluated must match what was meant to be taught.
  • Continuous: a regular part of teaching, not only at the end.
  • Comprehensive: covers knowledge, skills, attitudes and behaviour.
  • Variety of tools: tests, observation, checklists, projects.
  • Valid and reliable: measures what it claims and gives steady results.
  • Co-operative: teachers, students and parents take part.
  • Gives feedback and aims at improvement, not at punishing.
  • Integrated with teaching and learning.

Process (steps)

  • 1. Fix the objectives to be evaluated
  • 2. Plan learning experiences for them
  • 3. Select or prepare suitable tools
  • 4. Collect evidence (test, observe, project)
  • 5. Analyse and interpret the data
  • 6. Make judgments and decisions
  • 7. Give feedback and use it to improve teaching and learning
Continuous and comprehensive evaluation (CCE) 5 / 10

Continuous: evaluation is done regularly throughout the year, within teaching (unit tests, observation, assignments), so that problems are found early. Comprehensive: it covers all aspects of the child: scholastic and co-scholastic.

Importance

  • Reduces examination stress and the burden of one big test
  • Finds learning gaps early; allows remedial teaching
  • Gives a complete picture of the child, including skills and values
  • Uses many techniques, not only written tests
  • Teacher and student get regular feedback
  • Develops regular study habits; makes learning joyful
  • Helps in guidance and shows strengths for career choice
Grading system; grading vs marking 5

Grading places a student in a group (A, B, C…) according to a range of marks. Marking gives exact scores (73/100).

  • Advantages of grading: reduces unhealthy competition and stress; small differences in marks do not matter; error in marking has less effect; groups similar performances; includes co-scholastic areas easily.
  • Disadvantages: less precise; students on the border of two grades may be treated unequally; difficult for ranking; may reduce effort to improve.
  • Marking: precise and good for ranking, but encourages comparison and exam fear.
Role of parents and teachers feedback 5 / 2½+2½

Feedback is information about performance given so the learner can improve.

  • Teacher feedback: tells the student what is right, what is wrong and how to improve; timely, specific and positive; encourages effort; guides the next step.
  • Parent feedback: parents give information about the child at home (habits, interests, worries) and encourage learning there; support the child's study and attendance.
  • Together: parent-teacher meetings and progress reports give a full picture, build trust and allow joint action.

Good feedback is specific, timely, positive and focused on improvement; it should not shame the child.

Online examination: advantages and limitations 2½+2½ / 5
  • Advantages: quick scoring and results; saves paper and cost; can be taken from anywhere; flexible timing; question banks and randomised questions; instant feedback; less human error; records are stored.
  • Limitations: needs computers, internet and electricity; digital divide; risk of cheating and impersonation; technical failures; not suitable for all subjects (drawing, long answers); needs digital skills and security.
Open book examination: meaning, advantages, misconceptions 2+8 / 5

An examination in which students may use books or notes during the test. The questions test understanding and use of knowledge, not memory.

  • Advantages: reduces memory pressure and fear; tests higher-order thinking (analysis, application); teaches how to find and use information; closer to real life; reduces cheating.
  • Limitations: students may not prepare; time is wasted searching; hard to frame good questions; weak students may copy without thinking.

Misconceptions

  • "It is easy": it is usually harder, because answers cannot be copied from the book
  • "No preparation needed": students must know where and how to use material
  • "Anyone can pass": marks depend on application, not copying
  • "It encourages cheating": the questions are designed so that copying does not help

Revised Bloom Taxonomy of Educational Objectives

Asked in all 4 papers.

Revised (Anderson) taxonomy: cognitive domain 5

Anderson and Krathwohl (2001) revised Bloom's 1956 list. Names changed from nouns to action verbs and the top level changed. Think of climbing a staircase from simple to complex thinking.

  • Remember: recall facts. Define, list, name.
  • Understand: explain ideas in own words. Explain, summarise, give examples.
  • Apply: use knowledge in a new situation. Solve, demonstrate, use.
  • Analyse: break into parts and see relations. Compare, classify, differentiate.
  • Evaluate: judge using criteria. Justify, criticise, decide.
  • Create: put parts together to form something new. Design, plan, compose.

Changes: knowledge became Remember; comprehension became Understand; application became Apply; synthesis was replaced by Create and moved to the top; a knowledge dimension (factual, conceptual, procedural, metacognitive) was added.

Psychomotor domain 5

Objectives about physical skills and movement, such as drawing, handwriting, using instruments and sports. In steps from simple to complex (Simpson):

  • Perception: notices cues through the senses.
  • Set: gets ready to act.
  • Guided response: imitates under guidance.
  • Mechanism: performs with some confidence.
  • Complex overt response: performs skilfully and smoothly.
  • Adaptation: changes the skill for new situations.
  • Origination: creates new movements.
Affective domain (for the teacher's list) 5

Objectives about feelings, attitudes and values (Krathwohl), from simple to complex:

  • Receiving: pays attention.
  • Responding: reacts willingly.
  • Valuing: accepts a value.
  • Organising: builds a value system.
  • Characterising: lives by the values.

Tests and the Measuring Instrument

Good measuring instrument and achievement test are asked in 3 of 4 papers.

Characteristics of a good measuring instrument 10 / 5

A good instrument is like a good weighing scale: it measures the right thing, gives the same result each time and is easy to use.

  • Validity: it measures what it is meant to measure. A maths test with very hard English wording is not a valid test of maths. Factors lowering validity: unclear instructions, wrong difficulty, short test, unfair scoring.
  • Reliability: it gives steady, consistent results when repeated. Factors: length of the test, clear questions, time, scoring errors, student mood.
  • Objectivity: different scorers give the same score; free from personal bias. Objective type tests are most objective.
  • Practicability (usability): easy to administer, score and interpret; low cost; takes reasonable time.
  • Comprehensiveness: covers all the content and objectives in due proportion.
  • Discrimination: separates good from weak students.
  • Norms: scores can be compared with a reference group.
  • Economy and clarity: clear language, suitable difficulty, time-saving.
Achievement test: meaning, types, uses and importance 2+8 / 5

A test that measures how much a student has learnt in a subject after instruction.

Types

  • Oral, written and practical tests
  • Teacher-made and standardised tests
  • Essay type, short answer and objective type tests
  • Unit tests, term tests, annual examinations

Uses and importance

  • For students: know their level and weak areas; motivation; improvement.
  • For teachers: check teaching success; group students; plan remedial teaching; give marks and grades.
  • For the school and parents: report progress; promotion; selection; guidance.
  • For the curriculum: shows which content needs change.
Diagnostic test and prognostic test 2+3

Diagnostic test: finds the exact area and cause of a learner's difficulty. Like a doctor's check-up before treatment.

How it helps the teacher

  • Shows common errors and learning gaps
  • Helps plan remedial teaching and groups
  • Improves teaching methods and quality of learning
  • Gives students awareness of their weak points

Prognostic test: predicts how a student is likely to succeed in a course or future work.

  • Selecting students for courses
  • Educational and vocational guidance
  • Grouping students and planning teaching
  • Early identification of those at risk
Criterion referenced vs norm referenced test 5 / 2½+2½
  • Norm referenced: compares a student with other students (rank, percentile). Purpose: selection and ranking. Example: entrance test.
  • Criterion referenced: compares a student with a fixed standard of mastery (can solve 8 of 10 equations). Purpose: to know whether learning goals are achieved. Example: unit mastery test, driving test.
  • Difference: norm referenced asks "how well compared with others?"; criterion referenced asks "what can the student do?". Norm referenced uses average and rank; criterion referenced uses a cut-off score.
Standardised vs teacher-made tests 5 / 1+4
  • Standardised: prepared by experts; tested on large groups; fixed instructions, time and scoring; has norms; high reliability and validity; costly. Example: aptitude and intelligence tests.
  • Teacher-made: prepared by the class teacher for a particular class; follows the actual teaching; flexible and cheap; no norms; reliability and validity are limited.
Objective type, multiple choice and matching tests 1+4 / 5 / 5+5

Objective type tests have a single correct answer, so scoring is not affected by the scorer's opinion.

  • Advantages: scoring is fast and objective; covers a wide range of content; high reliability; students cannot bluff; can be reused.
  • Disadvantages: takes time to prepare; encourages guessing; tests recall more than expression; cannot test writing or creativity.

Framing a multiple choice test

  • Stem clear and complete; one correct answer
  • 3–4 distractors that look likely but are wrong
  • Options similar in length and form; avoid "all of the above" habits
  • Correct answers placed randomly

Framing a matching test

  • Two lists with a clear rule for matching
  • More responses than premises
  • Short, related items in each list; keep it to one page
  • Give clear instructions and the scoring
Written tests (essay, short answer) and performance tests 5 / 3+2
  • Written test: students answer in writing. Essay type: develops expression and organisation; but scoring is subjective and sampling of content is limited. Short answer: covers more content, quicker to score. Advantages: easy to prepare, tests thinking and writing. Limitations: subjective scoring, time-consuming, handwriting bias.
  • Performance test: students show a skill by doing it: experiments, drawing, speech, project work, practicals. Importance: tests skills that written tests cannot, links learning to real life, develops confidence, shows process as well as product.
  • Tools used: checklist, rating scale, rubric, observation schedule, portfolio.

Tools and Techniques of Evaluation

Questionnaire, checklist, rating scale, observation and rubric are asked often.

Techniques of evaluation 1+4

Techniques are of three kinds:

  • Tests: written, oral, practical, objective
  • Non-test techniques: observation, interview, questionnaire, checklist, rating scale, rubric, anecdotal record, portfolio, project
  • Others: sociometry, case study, self and peer assessment
Questionnaire: meaning, characteristics, how it helps 2+4+4 / 5

A set of written questions given to many persons to collect information about their opinions, habits, interests or problems.

Characteristics of a good questionnaire

  • Clear, short and simple questions
  • Questions related to the aim; no hints
  • Logical order, from easy to difficult
  • Neat and attractive; clear directions
  • Covers all points needed
  • Easy to answer and score
  • Questions that do not hurt or embarrass

As a tool of evaluation

  • Collects information from many students quickly and cheaply
  • Shows attitudes, interests, study habits and problems
  • Answers can be given freely and often honestly
  • Useful for evaluating non-test areas and for feedback on teaching
Observation, checklist and rating scale 2½+2½
  • Observation: watching behaviour in natural situations. Importance: shows real behaviour, suits young children and co-scholastic areas, no special tool needed, gives first-hand data. Limits: time-consuming, observer bias.
  • Checklist: a list of points marked "yes/no" (seen or not seen). Uses: evaluating skills in practicals, project steps, habits and behaviour; simple and quick.
  • Rating scale: records the degree of a trait on a scale (for example 1 to 5: always, often, sometimes, rarely, never). Uses: judging qualities like leadership, speech, neatness; gives more detail than a checklist; helps in comparing students.
Rubric: meaning and steps of construction 1+4

A rubric is a scoring guide that lists the criteria for a task and describes performance at each level (excellent, good, fair, poor). It tells students beforehand what is expected.

Steps

  • Decide the task and the learning objective
  • List the criteria (for example content, accuracy, presentation)
  • Fix the levels of performance (3 to 5 levels)
  • Write a clear description for each criterion at each level
  • Give marks or points to each level
  • Try it out and revise; share it with students
Interview (teacher's list) 5

A planned face-to-face conversation to gather information. Types: structured (fixed questions), unstructured (free flow), semi-structured, individual and group. Uses: knowing interests, problems and attitudes; guidance; selection; builds rapport.

Statistics: Concepts

The numerical questions are in the Worked Solutions tab.

Meaning and importance of statistics in education 5

Statistics is the science of collecting, organising, presenting, analysing and interpreting numerical data.

  • Presents results of tests in a short, clear way
  • Compares students, classes and methods
  • Finds averages and spread of performance
  • Shows relationships (correlation) between abilities
  • Helps in grading, guidance, research and decision-making
  • Helps judge the quality of tests (reliability, validity)
Frequency distribution: steps 5 / 4
  • Find the range = highest score − lowest score
  • Decide the number of classes (usually 8 to 15)
  • Find class interval size = range ÷ number of classes (round to a convenient number)
  • Write the class intervals starting from the lowest score
  • Put a tally mark for each score in its class
  • Count the tallies to get the frequency of each class and check that the total equals N

Advantages: shortens a long list of scores, shows the pattern, and helps to calculate averages and draw graphs.

Graphical representation: uses; histogram and frequency polygon 2+4+4 / 5

Uses

  • Makes data easy to see and understand
  • Shows comparison and trends quickly
  • Attracts interest and helps memory
  • Shows the shape of the distribution

Histogram: steps

  • Mark class boundaries on the X-axis and frequency on the Y-axis
  • Draw a bar for each class with width = class size and height = frequency
  • Bars touch each other

Frequency polygon: steps

  • Find the mid-point of each class
  • Plot a point (mid-point, frequency) for each class
  • Add one empty class at each end with frequency 0
  • Join the points with straight lines
Measures of central tendency: mean, median, mode 1+4 / 2+6+2

A single value that represents the whole group.

  • Mean: the arithmetic average = Σfx ÷ N. Uses every score.
  • Median: the middle value when scores are arranged in order.
  • Mode: the most frequent score.

Why mean is the best: it uses all the scores, is easy to understand, is clearly defined by a formula, is suitable for further calculation (SD, correlation) and is stable from sample to sample. Its weakness is that extreme scores affect it.

Measures of variability: range, quartile deviation, standard deviation 1+4
  • Range: highest − lowest score. Quick, but uses only two scores.
  • Quartile deviation (QD): QD = (Q3 − Q1) ÷ 2; the spread of the middle 50%. Not affected by extreme scores.
  • Standard deviation (SD): the square root of the average of squared deviations from the mean: σ = √(Σd² ÷ N). Most reliable measure of spread.

A small SD means scores are close to the mean (a homogeneous group); a large SD means they are spread out.

Correlation: meaning and types 1+3 / 2

Correlation shows the degree of relationship between two sets of scores, for example marks in English and Kannada. The coefficient of correlation lies from −1 to +1.

  • Positive: both increase together (study time and marks).
  • Negative: one increases as the other decreases.
  • Zero: no relationship.

Interpretation

  • 0.00 to ±0.20: negligible
  • ±0.20 to ±0.40: low
  • ±0.40 to ±0.70: moderate
  • ±0.70 to ±0.90: high
  • ±0.90 to ±1.00: very high

Methods: rank difference (Spearman) ρ = 1 − 6ΣD² ÷ N(N² − 1), and product moment (Pearson). Correlation does not prove that one thing causes the other.

Normal probability curve (NPC): characteristics and uses 5 / 1+4

A bell-shaped, symmetrical curve showing how many chance events and measures of human traits (marks, height) are spread: most near the average, few at the extremes.

  • Bell-shaped and symmetrical about the centre
  • Mean, median and mode are equal and lie at the centre
  • Highest point (maximum ordinate) at the mean
  • The curve is asymptotic: it comes close to the base line but never touches it
  • Points of inflection at ±1 SD from the mean
  • About 68% of cases lie within ±1 SD, 95% within ±2 SD and 99.7% within ±3 SD
  • Total area under the curve is 1 (or 100%)
  • Skewness is zero

Uses: grading on a curve, comparing scores, judging the difficulty of a test, and finding how many students fall above or below a score. Draw a neat bell curve with the mean at the middle and ±1, ±2, ±3 SD marked on the base line.

Skewness and its types 1+4 / 2½+2½

Skewness shows how far a distribution is not symmetrical.

  • Positive skewness: the long tail is on the right; most scores are low; mean > median > mode. The test was difficult.
  • Negative skewness: the long tail is on the left; most scores are high; mean < median < mode. The test was easy.
  • Zero skewness: symmetrical, as in the normal curve.

Uses: tells about the difficulty of a test and the nature of a group's performance.

Standard deviation here is σ = √(Σd² ÷ N). Rank correlation is ρ = 1 − 6ΣD² ÷ N(N² − 1), with tied scores given the average rank.

Mean, median, mode: N = 110 (Nov 2023) 2+6+2

Classes 0–10 … 60–70; f = 7, 13, 24, 26, 18, 12, 10. Mid-points: 5, 15, 25, 35, 45, 55, 65.

Mean: Σfx = 35 + 195 + 600 + 910 + 810 + 660 + 650 = 3860. Mean = 3860 ÷ 110 = 35.09.

Median: N/2 = 55. Cumulative frequency: 7, 20, 44, 70, so the median class is 30–40 (L = 30, cf = 44, f = 26, i = 10). Median = 30 + (55 − 44)/26 × 10 = 34.23.

Mode: highest frequency 26 in class 30–40 (f0 = 24, f2 = 18). Mode = 30 + (26 − 24)/(2×26 − 24 − 18) × 10 = 32.

Interpretation: mean (35.09) > median (34.23) > mode (32): slightly positively skewed. Most students scored between 20 and 50; the average is about 35.

Quartile deviation: scores (Nov/Dec 2024) 5

Scores: 10, 12, 16, 18, 20, 21, 22, 25 (N = 8).

QD = (21.75 − 13)/2 = 4.38.

Difficulty level: Q2 − Q1 = 6 and Q3 − Q2 = 2.75. The median is nearer to Q3, so scores bunch at the higher end: the test was easy for this group.

Ranks (highest = 1, ties share the average rank). Science 58, 42, 47, 58, 60, 45, 48 → 2.5, 7, 5, 2.5, 1, 6, 4. Mathematics 50, 38, 42, 36, 38, 46, 38 → 1, 5, 3, 7, 5, 2, 5.

Correlation: Test-I and Test-II, N = 8 (Oct/Nov 2023) 1+3+6
