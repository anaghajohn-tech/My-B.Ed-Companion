Creating an Inclusive School

Every topic asked in the 5 papers (2018 – 2024), grouped and explained. No official answer key was available, so these are model answers.

Inclusive Education

Appears in all 5 papers.

Meaning and importance 2+4

Education in which children with and without disabilities or other special needs learn together in the same regular classroom, with the support they need.

Importance

  • Equal right to education for every child
  • Removes stigma and builds acceptance of diversity
  • Helps social and emotional growth of all learners
  • Gives children with special needs real-life peers and models
  • Makes better use of resources than separate schools
Advantages and limitations 4+4

Advantages

  • Better academic and social outcomes for children with special needs
  • All children learn tolerance, empathy and cooperation
  • Fewer labels; less segregation
  • Family and community involvement
  • Lower cost than special schools

Limitations

  • Shortage of trained teachers and support staff
  • Large classes and little individual attention
  • Inaccessible buildings and lack of aids
  • Negative attitudes of teachers, peers or parents
  • Rigid curriculum and examinations

Barriers in Inclusive Education

Social and attitudinal barriers asked every year.

Types of barriers 5
  • Social: stigma, discrimination, poverty, caste or gender bias, over-protection by families
  • Attitudinal: low expectations, fear, pity, belief that special children cannot learn or will slow the class
  • Educational: rigid curriculum, exams, lack of trained teachers, teaching aids and support services
  • Physical: no ramps, toilets or transport; poor classroom layout
How to overcome them 4
  • Awareness programmes for teachers, parents and peers
  • Teacher training in inclusive methods
  • Flexible curriculum, adapted tests and extra time
  • Barrier-free buildings, aids and assistive technology
  • Positive role models, success stories, peer support
  • Strict implementation of RTE and the disability laws

Early Detection and Functional Assessment

Early detection in 4 papers; functional assessment in 4.

Importance of early detection 5

Spotting a disability or learning difficulty as early as possible.

  • Early help gives the best results because the brain learns fastest in childhood
  • Prevents the problem from becoming worse
  • Reduces failure, drop-out and low self-esteem
  • Allows early planning of school and family support
  • Saves cost of later treatment
Functional assessment of SEN: importance and steps 5

Finds out what a child can actually do in daily and school activities, and what support is needed, rather than only labelling the disability.

Importance

  • Plans suitable teaching and support
  • Shows strengths as well as needs
  • Guides the Individualised Education Plan (IEP)
  • Tracks progress

Steps

  • Referral and observation
  • Collect information from parents, teachers and records
  • Assess skills: academic, communication, motor, social, daily living
  • Identify needs and set goals
  • Plan teaching and support; review regularly

Inclusive Classroom and Teacher

Appeared in 4 papers.

Role of the inclusive classroom 5
  • Welcoming, accessible and friendly environment
  • Flexible seating, clear routines and visual supports
  • Cooperative learning and peer support
  • Varied materials and methods to suit all learners
  • Respects every child and celebrates differences
Role of the inclusive teacher 5
  • Identifies learning needs early and refers when needed
  • Plans differentiated lessons and adapts the curriculum
  • Uses aids, ICT and peer tutoring
  • Keeps high expectations and positive attitude
  • Works with special educators, parents and counsellors
  • Assesses fairly with flexible methods

Disabilities and Special Needs

Visual impairment, learning disability and cerebral palsy are most frequent.

Sensory disabled children; visual impairment 2+3+5

Sensory disabled children have loss of one or more senses: vision or hearing.

Characteristics of visual impairment

  • Holds books very close or tilts head
  • Rubs eyes, squints, complains of headaches
  • Trips on objects; poor eye-hand coordination
  • Cannot see the board; avoids visual tasks
  • Depends on touch and hearing

Accommodating in class

  • Seat near the board, good lighting
  • Large print, Braille, audio books, screen readers
  • Read aloud what is written; describe visuals
  • Tactile models, clear verbal instructions
  • Extra time, a buddy for support
Sensory (hearing) and locomotor disabilities: specifics 5

Hearing

  • Front seat, face the child, clear speech
  • Sign language, written notes, visual aids, captions

Locomotor

  • Ramps and accessible toilets
  • Adapted desks, assistive devices
  • Extra time for writing; scribe or typing
  • Include in sports through adapted activities
Learning disability: dysgraphia and dyscalculia 1+2+2

Learning disability is a neurological difficulty in reading, writing or maths, despite normal intelligence.

Dysgraphia

  • Poor, slow, untidy handwriting
  • Spacing and spelling errors; avoids writing

Dyscalculia

  • Weak number sense; mixes signs and place value
  • Trouble with tables, time, money and word problems
Cerebral palsy; autism 5 each

Cerebral palsy

A group of permanent movement and posture disorders caused by damage to the developing brain.

  • Stiff or floppy muscles, poor balance
  • Difficulty in walking, writing and speech
  • May also have epilepsy or learning difficulty
  • Intelligence varies; many learn well with support

Autism

  • Difficulty with communication and social interaction
  • Repetitive behaviours, strong routines
  • Sensitive to sound, light or touch
  • Narrow, intense interests
Developmental and neuro-developmental disabilities; multiple disabilities 5
  • Developmental: delayed development of speech, learning, movement or social skills (e.g. intellectual disability)
  • Neuro-developmental: brain-based, such as autism, ADHD, learning disabilities
  • Multiple disabilities: two or more together; need an individual plan, step-by-step adaptation, assistive devices and teamwork of teacher, special educator and therapists

Gifted Children

Asked in 3 papers.

Educational programmes for gifted children 5–8

Gifted children show very high ability in one or more areas.

  • Enrichment: extra projects, research, challenging tasks within the class
  • Acceleration: skipping a grade or subject
  • Ability grouping and pull-out classes
  • Mentoring by experts; clubs, Olympiads, competitions
  • Independent study and ICT resources
  • Counselling for emotional needs; avoid boredom

Differentiation, Strategies and Adaptation

Differentiated instruction in all 5 papers.

Differentiated instruction: meaning, content and process 3+7

Teaching that adjusts what, how and how fast students learn, to suit their different readiness, interests and learning styles.

Modifying content

  • Simplified or enriched texts at different levels
  • Tiered tasks; choice of topics
  • Visual, audio and hands-on materials

Modifying process

  • Flexible grouping, peer tutoring, learning stations
  • Different methods: demonstration, discussion, role play
  • Extra time, scaffolding and teacher support
  • Product and assessment choices (project, oral, written)

Merits / limitations

Merits: meets individual needs, raises engagement. Limitations: demands time, training and resources.

Inclusive strategies for diverse needs 5
  • Cooperative learning and peer tutoring
  • Co-teaching and team teaching
  • Universal Design for Learning
  • Multi-sensory and activity-based teaching
  • Use of ICT and assistive devices
  • Individualised Education Plan; flexible assessment
Curricular and co-curricular adaptation; accommodation and modification 5
  • Accommodation: changes in how a child learns or is tested without changing standards (extra time, large print, scribe)
  • Modification: changes in what is expected (simpler content or fewer outcomes)
  • Curricular adaptation: adjust objectives, content, methods, materials and evaluation
  • Co-curricular: adapt rules and roles in games, drama, music and trips so every child can take part; mixed groups; assistive equipment

Universal Design for Learning (UDL)

Appeared in all 5 papers.

Meaning and components 1+4

A framework for planning lessons so that they work for all learners from the start, instead of adding adaptations later.

Three principles / components

  • Multiple means of engagement (the why): interest, choice, motivation, persistence
  • Multiple means of representation (the what): text, audio, video, diagrams, hands-on
  • Multiple means of action and expression (the how): speak, write, draw, build, use technology

Example: a science concept taught with a video, a model and a text, and assessed through a poster, talk or written answer.

Peer Mediated Instruction and CWPT

Appeared in all 5 papers.

Peer mediated instruction (PMI): meaning, advantages, types 2+2

Students teach and support each other under the teacher's guidance.

Advantages

  • Active learning; more practice and feedback
  • Builds friendships and acceptance
  • Raises confidence of both tutor and learner
  • Frees the teacher to help individuals

Types

  • Class Wide Peer Tutoring (CWPT)
  • Cross-age tutoring
  • Peer assisted learning strategies (PALS)
  • Cooperative learning; buddy system / circle of friends
Class Wide Peer Tutoring (CWPT) in inclusive classrooms 6
  • Class is divided into pairs or two teams
  • Pairs of mixed ability take turns as tutor and tutee
  • Teacher gives structured material (word lists, maths facts)
  • Tutor presents, tutee answers; correct answers earn points, errors are corrected
  • Roles switch; teams are praised for points
  • Teacher monitors and gives feedback

Benefit: every child, including those with special needs, takes an active role.

Co-teaching and Team Teaching

Appeared in all 5 papers.

Co-teaching methods 2+4+4

Two teachers (usually a general and a special educator) share planning, teaching and assessment of one class.

  • One teach, one observe: one leads, the other collects data
  • One teach, one assist: one leads, the other helps individuals
  • Station teaching: class split into groups that rotate
  • Parallel teaching: each teaches half the class the same content
  • Alternative teaching: one teaches a small group while the other teaches the rest
  • Team teaching: both teach together, sharing the lesson
Team teaching: meaning and method 1+4

A group of teachers plan, deliver and evaluate a lesson together.

  • Plan together: objectives, content, roles
  • Each teacher presents the part he or she is strongest in
  • Teachers support and add to each other during the lesson
  • Joint evaluation and feedback

Benefits: combines expertise, better attention to all learners. Limits: needs time to plan and good understanding between teachers.

Laws and Policies

RTE, PWD Act 1995 and the 2006 policy come up almost every year.

Provisions for inclusive education as per RTE Act 2009 5
  • Free and compulsory education for ages 6–14 in the neighbourhood school
  • No child can be denied admission, held back or expelled
  • 25% seats for disadvantaged groups in private schools; children with disabilities are included in this group
  • No discrimination; special training for out-of-school children
  • Barrier-free access, trained teachers, School Management Committee
Persons with Disabilities Act 1995 5

Persons with Disabilities (Equal Opportunities, Protection of Rights and Full Participation) Act, 1995.

  • Free education up to age 18 in an appropriate environment
  • Integration of children with disabilities into mainstream schools
  • Prevention and early detection; transport, aids and appliances, scholarships
  • Barrier-free schools; curriculum and exam modification; teacher training
  • 3% reservation in government jobs; non-discrimination
National Policy for Persons with Disabilities 2006 5
  • Aims at equal opportunities, protection of rights and full participation
  • Focus on prevention, early detection and intervention
  • Physical, educational and economic rehabilitation
  • Special attention to women and children with disabilities
  • Barrier-free environment and social security; inclusive education in mainstream schools
Special provisions for the education of disadvantaged groups 5

Groups include SC/ST, girls, minorities, poor, children with disabilities and street or migrant children.

  • Free textbooks, uniforms, scholarships, mid-day meal
  • Residential schools and hostels (e.g. KGBV, Ashram schools)
  • Reservation of seats; special coaching and bridge courses
  • Neighbourhood schools, transport and flexible timings
  • Awareness and incentives for parents

ICT in Inclusive Education

Appeared in 4 papers.

Using ICT for inclusive academic instruction 5
  • Visual impairment: screen readers, text-to-speech, Braille displays, audio books
  • Hearing impairment: captions, video with sign language, visual presentations
  • Physical disability: adapted keyboards, voice input, eye-gaze tools
  • Learning disability: speech-to-text, spell check, interactive apps and games
  • Digital, accessible content; online and blended learning
  • Smartboards and multimedia keep all learners engaged
